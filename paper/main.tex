\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Atmospheric drag can limit the orbital lifetime of small spacecraft, while their size and power budgets constrain propulsion hardware. This paper presents an electron propulsion concept that uses the ionosphere to supply its return current, eliminating the need for stored propellant. A cathode biased below the spacecraft body emits an electron beam through an aperture; the body charges positively and collects ambient electrons to replenish the emitted charge. Particle-in-cell simulations (WarpX) of conducting bodies 10\,mm in diameter predict 3.4-43\,nN of beam thrust at the tested supply voltages of 100-350\,V, with beam-supply powers of 12-278\,mW and escape fractions of 96-99\,\%. A model calibrated to these simulations and coupled to year-long orbital and ionospheric calculations estimates the power required for drag compensation. For a body 10\,mm in diameter and 30\,mm long in sun-synchronous orbits at 500-600\,km, the estimated mean beam-supply demand is 10-46\,mW near solar maximum and 0.4-2\,mW near solar minimum. The architecture combines electron emission with ambient current collection in the spacecraft's own structure, offering a compact propulsion approach for missions with nanonewton thrust requirements.
\end{abstract}
\vspace{0.4cm}
]

\section{Introduction}

Conventional gridded ion thrusters require stored propellant, a feed system and a neutralizer \cite{nasasoa26}. This hardware adds mass, volume and integration complexity. This paper investigates a simpler architecture: emit electrons and let the ionosphere provide the return current.

The tradeoff is thrust per watt. For a directed exhaust, $F/P = 2\eta/v_e$, where $\eta$ is the electrical-to-jet efficiency and $v_e$ is the exhaust speed. Electrons accelerated through a few hundred volts reach high exhaust speeds, giving little momentum per unit energy. The simulated 200\,V operating point delivers about 0.2\,\textmu N/W, making electron propulsion power-intensive at millinewton thrust levels.

At the nanonewton drag levels examined here, the beam-supply demand is milliwatts. A cathode, an aperture, a high-voltage supply and the spacecraft's conducting skin provide propulsion without stored propellant. The aim is to reduce the mass, volume and integration complexity of station-keeping hardware on small spacecraft.

\section{Concept}

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{imgs/concept_steps_mpl.pdf}
\caption{Operating sequence. The supply is switched on, the cathode emits, the escaping beam charges the body positive, and the positive body collects ambient electrons until collection equals emission.}
\label{fig:concept}
\end{figure*}

A cathode held at $-V$ with respect to the spacecraft body emits electrons and accelerates them out through an aperture (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}). The escaping beam carries directed momentum and produces reaction thrust. Each escaping electron leaves the body one charge more positive, so the body's conducting skin collects ambient electrons from the plasma, and the supply returns them to the cathode. The body approaches the floating potential $\varphi$, measured relative to the ambient plasma, where net collection balances beam escape.

The current loop relies on an energy asymmetry: accelerated beam electrons can escape the positive body's potential well, while low-energy ambient electrons are attracted to it. Ambient electrons arrive over the collecting surface rather than as a directed counter-beam. Thus the same spacecraft structure acts as both the accelerating electrode and the return-current collector.

\section{Theory}

\begingroup
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{5pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{5pt}
\setlength{\abovedisplayshortskip}{0pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayshortskip}{3pt}
The propulsion mechanism couples the momentum carried by the emitted beam to the current collected from the surrounding plasma.

Thrust is the momentum flux of the escaping beam:
\begin{equation}
F = \frac{I_{\mathrm{esc}}\sqrt{2m_e\,\mathrm{KE}}}{e}.
\label{eq:thrust}
\end{equation}
Here $I_{\mathrm{esc}} = \fesc I$ is the escaping part of the emitted current; collimated exhaust is assumed.

The energy each electron actually leaves with is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KE} = \kappa\,e\,(V-\varphi),
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with KE in joules and $V$ the supply voltage.

Jet power divided by electrical beam-supply power is
\begin{equation}
\eta = \frac{P_{\mathrm{jet}}}{P}
     = \kappa\,\frac{V-\varphi}{V}\,\fesc.
\label{eq:efficiency}
\end{equation}
Throughout, $P = VI$ denotes power delivered by the supply between body and cathode. Total spacecraft power also depends on emitter technology and converter efficiency.

The energy fraction $\kappa$ relates exhaust energy to the available potential drop; ideal emission at cathode potential gives $\kappa \approx 1$. The floating potential $\varphi$ depends on collecting area, body shape and plasma conditions. The ratio $\varphi/V$ is the fraction of supply voltage spent overcoming the body's positive potential. The escape fraction $\fesc$ is the beam fraction that clears the body, controlled by the exit-aperture geometry.

\noindent\textbf{Control.} The electrical operating point provides two useful control signals:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Thrust estimation.} Measured current, supply voltage and body potential relative to the plasma give a thrust estimate through Eqs.~\ref{eq:thrust}--\ref{eq:energy}, with calibrated escape and divergence corrections. An in-flight thrust stand is unnecessary.
  \item \textbf{Feedback.} A microcontroller can adjust the supply voltage to track the thrust command using the measured current and floating potential. Density, temperature and day/night changes enter through these signals, without an explicit ionosphere model or lookup table.
\end{enumerate}
This is a proposed control principle; flight implementation requires current and plasma-potential sensing and calibration of the thrust estimate.
\par\endgroup

\section{Particle-in-cell simulations}

The device is simulated with WarpX \cite{warpx22,amrex19} in axisymmetric (RZ) electrostatic mode. The slender body is the main case for the simulation results and station-keeping analysis; the compact body provides the verification-ladder reference and a geometry comparison. Both demonstrate the same propulsion mechanism. Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} shows their shared internal gun geometry, and Table~\ref{tab:setup} gives the simulation parameters.

The acceleration gap is 4.7\,mm in both configurations. Extending the spacecraft body from 5.5 to 30.5\,mm increases the return-current collecting area without enlarging the acceleration region or frontal area. The compact body is the smallest complete electrode-and-collector geometry tested here; a minimum size has not been established. These dimensions characterize the electrodes and collecting structure; supply and emitter packaging depend on the flight implementation.

The body potential evolves self-consistently from the net charge emitted and collected, divided by the body's self-capacitance. Open boundaries and ambient-particle replenishment represent the surrounding ionosphere. A nine-stage verification sequence builds from a vacuum electron gun to the full device, checking emission, collection and floating-body coupling against theory and fixed criteria on escape, current balance and momentum; thermal collection agrees with theory within 1\,\%.

Figure~\ref{fig:dashboard} shows the slender-body simulation at 200\,V: the beam density at the final time, beam fates, thrust and beam-supply power. Table~\ref{tab:envelope} summarizes the sampled operating points. The fitted thrust relation is $F = 3.27\,I\sqrt{\mathrm{KE}}$ (with $F$ in nN, emitted $I$ in mA and KE in eV; the slope includes interception and divergence), with an energy calibration $\kappa \approx 0.81$. Increasing the body length multiplies its collecting area by 3.5: at 200\,V, the floating potential falls from 17.0 to 4.4\,V and thrust rises from 13.65 to 14.22\,nN. Collected-particle momentum offsets 0.4-1.4\,\% of beam thrust in the 200-350\,V cases. A separate 3D comparison with the geomagnetic field across the beam at flight strength changes thrust by under 1\,\%.

The fitted $\kappa$ reflects the numerical launch position: electrons are introduced 0.3\,mm above the cathode, missing part of the accelerating potential drop. A vacuum field calculation places this plane about 19\,\% of $V$ above cathode potential; the simulated exhaust-energy deficit is 17-18\,\% of $V$ in the cases of Table~\ref{tab:envelope}. Emission at cathode potential is therefore expected to increase $\kappa$ toward the ideal limit of one. Direct PIC confirmation is still required. All reported performance and mission estimates retain the existing calibration.

\section{Station keeping}

The station-keeping calculations use the slender body, with its collection prefactor fitted to the 200 and 350\,V slender PIC cases. The mission geometry approximates the 30.5\,mm simulated body as a 10\,mm diameter, 30\,mm long cylinder of mass 3.1\,g, flown with its axis along the velocity so that the thrust axis and low-drag pose coincide.

Drag and the plasma along the orbit come from a year-long TudatPy propagation \cite{tudat} with NRLMSISE-00 density \cite{msis} driven by measured solar and geomagnetic indices and IRI-2020 plasma \cite{iri} along the orbit, near solar maximum (2024) and minimum (2019). With the thruster on, ideal drag cancellation defines the thrust demand at each 5-minute point; the supply voltage follows it at the least beam power, with the floating potential estimated from the local plasma using the simulation-calibrated collection model. With the thruster off, the orbit decays freely. These estimates extend the calibration to voltages and plasma conditions beyond the PIC samples; 350\,V marks the highest tested voltage, not a physical limit of the concept.

Table~\ref{tab:mission} gives the model results. Without the thruster the 3\,g body re-enters from 400\,km in 46 days and from 550\,km in 11 months near solar maximum. At 500-700\,km, the mean thrust demand is 0.7-9\,nN, with mean beam-supply demand of 2.5-46\,mW near maximum and at most 2\,mW near minimum. At 400\,km near maximum the 38\,nN mean is below the 43\,nN simulated at 350\,V, but the thin night-side plasma pushes nearly half the points above the tested voltage range; near minimum the mean demand is 21\,mW. The ISS and equatorial orbits differ from the sun-synchronous one by about 10\,\%.

Over each orbit the demand changes by $1.6$-$2.0\times$ between the sunlit and eclipsed halves at 500-700\,km, and on the geomagnetic-storm days of 11 May, 12 August, 7-8 and 10-11 October 2024 the daily mean reaches $2.2$-$3.5\times$ the annual mean, the larger factors at the higher altitudes. A separate altitude-hold calculation caps the drive at 350\,V to test operation within the sampled voltage range. Temporary thrust deficits produce altitude dips of less than 10\,m at 500-700\,km over the modeled year.

For comparable exhaust energy and collection performance, the required current and beam power scale with the drag area. The modeled 1U CubeSat has 103 times the can's drag area and therefore needs 103 times its thrust. Applying the same factor to power gives a first-order estimate of about 1\,W at 600\,km near solar maximum and tens of milliwatts near minimum. Collection at this larger scale requires separate validation. The 1U lives longer in free fall because of its larger mass-to-drag-area ratio: 167 days from 400\,km near solar maximum, against 46 for the can.

\section{Conclusion}
A cathode, a high-voltage supply and the spacecraft's conducting skin form an electron propulsion system that replenishes emitted charge from the ionosphere. The simulated geometries produce 3.4-43\,nN at beam-supply powers of 12-278\,mW. The compact-to-slender comparison shows how collecting area can increase around an unchanged acceleration region. Mission calculations for the slender body indicate station keeping for a 3\,g spacecraft at 500-700\,km with milliwatt mean beam-supply demand. The contribution is a compact propulsion architecture in which the spacecraft structure closes the current loop. Cathode-potential emission, long-time settling and collection at larger spacecraft scales remain the principal simulation questions for extending the quantitative results.
