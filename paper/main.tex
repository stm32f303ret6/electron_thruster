\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Chip-scale spacecraft lose their orbit to atmospheric drag fastest of all: a 3\,g body 10\,mm across and 30\,mm long re-enters from 400\,km in 41--46 days and from 550\,km within a year near solar maximum, and no flown propulsion system is small enough to carry. This paper describes a thruster with no tank, no feed system and no neutralizer. A cathode held a few hundred volts below the spacecraft body emits electrons through a single aperture. The escaping beam produces thrust and leaves the body positively charged, so the body collects electrons from the ionospheric plasma until collection equals emission. The circuit closes through the ionosphere with zero net mass flow. Particle-in-cell simulations (WarpX) of a 10\,mm conducting body in a dayside ionospheric plasma measure the complete operating envelope over 100--350\,V: 3.4 to 43\,nN of thrust from 12 to 278\,mW of beam power, with 96--99\,\% of the beam escaping, a floating potential of 4--48\,V, and an energy efficiency of 0.68--0.77. Two measured constants describe every run to within 3\,\%. Year-long orbit propagations at 400--700\,km on near-equatorial, ISS and sun-synchronous orbits, near solar maximum (2024) and minimum (2019), with the supply voltage following the drag and the floating potential solved from the plasma along the orbit, show the 3\,g body holding 500--700\,km for a mean beam power of 2.5--48\,mW at solar maximum and 0.1--2\,mW at minimum, inside the tested 100--350\,V envelope at least 97\,\% of the time and never more than 10\,m below its target altitude; at 400\,km it needs 21\,mW at solar minimum, while near maximum the demand exceeds the simulated envelope. Because both drag and electron collection scale with area, the feasibility condition depends on shape, not size: a 3U CubeSat of the same shape needs about 1\,W at 600\,km near solar maximum and tens of milliwatts near minimum, an estimate in a sheath regime not yet simulated. A bench experiment confirms the electrode topology on hardware. A three-dimensional follow-up campaign puts the geomagnetic field perpendicular to the beam, the orientation the mission flies in: at flight strength the operating point survives (thrust within 0.8\,\%, escape unchanged, a 2.6\,V float shift at the settled equilibrium), while at ten times flight strength the return circuit cannot close and the device must fire along the field. Open items are the choice of flight emitter and the CubeSat sheath regime.
\end{abstract}
\end{center}

\section{Introduction}

Small spacecraft below 600\,km lose their orbits to atmospheric drag within months to a few years, and the smallest lose them fastest: drag grows with area and inertia with volume. Near solar maximum a 3\,g chip-scale body, 10\,mm across and 30\,mm long, re-enters from 400\,km in 41--46 days and from 550\,km in 300--329 days, while a 4\,kg body of the same shape at CubeSat size lasts about a year from 400\,km (Sec.~\ref{sec:mission}). Most carry no propulsion at all.

The thrust to hold altitude is nanonewtons to a few micronewtons, well within the range of flight-proven micropropulsion. Colloid thrusters on LISA Pathfinder resolved 0.1\,\textmu N \cite{anderson18}; NanoFEEP flew on a 1U CubeSat at 0.2--0.3\,W \cite{bock18}. Every one of these systems spends a stored propellant through tanks, valves, feed lines or precision emitter heads. The propellant imposes a total-impulse limit, and the hardware adds mass and cost that are rarely justified on a CubeSat.

This paper describes a thruster with none of that hardware. A cathode held below the spacecraft potential emits electrons through an aperture. The escaping beam produces thrust and leaves the body positively charged, so the body collects ambient electrons from the ionospheric plasma until collection balances emission. The circuit closes through the ionosphere with no propellant and no net mass flow. An electron beam carries about 200 times less momentum per watt than an ion beam, but LEO drag on a small spacecraft is nanonewtons, and at that scale the beam costs tens of milliwatts. The whole device is a cathode, an aperture and a high-voltage supply. It carries no propellant, no tank and no feed system. On a chip-scale spacecraft, where nothing flown fits, it holds 500--700\,km on milliwatts that the body's own solar cells can supply; the same device on a CubeSat needs watts.

The following sections describe the operating principle, test each claim through a ladder of particle-in-cell simulations that build from a vacuum electron gun to the full device in ionospheric plasma, and assess the result against a year of real drag at 400--700\,km on three orbit families, at both ends of the solar cycle, and against the lifetime of the same spacecraft without the thruster.

\section{Concept}
\label{sec:concept}

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{imgs/concept_steps_mpl.pdf}
\caption{Operating sequence. The supply is switched on, the cathode emits, the escaping beam charges the body positive, and the positive body collects ambient electrons until collection equals emission.}
\label{fig:concept}
\end{figure*}

The device has three parts (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}):
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item a cathode, held at $-V$ with respect to the spacecraft body, that emits electrons and accelerates them out through an aperture;
  \item the spacecraft's own conducting skin, which is the second electrode and collects electrons from the plasma;
  \item a high-voltage supply between the two, which moves collected electrons back to the cathode.
\end{enumerate}

Once switched on, the device reaches a steady state:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item the spacecraft floats in the ionospheric plasma;
  \item the supply is switched on and the cathode emits;
  \item each escaping electron leaves the body one charge more positive;
  \item the positive body attracts electrons from the plasma;
  \item collection grows until it equals emission. The body settles at a potential $\varphi$ where the two currents balance.
\end{enumerate}

Two questions decide whether this works.

Why does the positive body not recapture its own beam? Because the beam electrons carry 100--300\,eV while $\varphi$ is a few volts to a few tens of volts; ambient electrons at 0.1\,eV are captured, beam electrons are not.

Does the returning current cancel the thrust? The collected electrons gain 4--48\,eV from the floating potential, against 77--272\,eV for the beam, and they arrive from all directions rather than straight astern. The simulations measure the net cancellation at 0.4--1.4\,\% of the beam thrust at 200--350\,V, rising to 9\,\% at 100\,V where the slower beam carries less momentum relative to the collected electrons (Sec.~\ref{sec:envelope}).

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Thrust}
The beam is a current $I$ of electrons with kinetic energy $\mathrm{KE}$. Its momentum flux is the thrust:
\begin{equation}
F = \frac{I\sqrt{2 m_e\,\mathrm{KE}}}{e}
  = 3.37\;\frac{\mathrm{nN}}{\mathrm{mA}\sqrt{\mathrm{eV}}}\; I\sqrt{\mathrm{KE}}.
\label{eq:thrust}
\end{equation}
The constant contains only the electron mass and charge. The exhaust velocity is $v_e = \sqrt{2e\,\mathrm{KE}/m_e}$, of order $10^7$\,m/s.

\subsection{The float tax}
The body floats at $+\varphi$ and the cathode at $\varphi - V$, so the accelerating potential is $V-\varphi$. Space charge near the cathode depresses it further. The exhaust energy is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KE} = \kappa\,(V-\varphi),
\label{eq:ke}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the gun's energy transmission, set by how hard the gun is loaded relative to its space-charge limit. The ratio $\varphi/V$ is the fraction of supply voltage spent on collecting the return current.

\subsection{Power and efficiency}
Beam power is $P = VI$. Jet power over supply power is
\begin{equation}
\eta = \kappa\,\frac{V-\varphi}{V}\,\fesc ,
\label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
with $\fesc$ the fraction of the beam that clears the body. Eliminating $I$ between Eq.~\eqref{eq:thrust} and $P=VI$ at fixed thrust gives $P \propto F\sqrt{V}$: doubling the voltage costs about 40\,\% more power for the same thrust. Thrust per watt is $F/P \approx 3.37\sqrt{\kappa}/\sqrt{V}$ in \textmu N/W with $V$ in volts, about 0.2\,\textmu N/W at 200\,V.

\subsection{Emission ceiling}
Low voltage is cheap, but the current a gun can source is limited by space charge. The planar Child--Langmuir scale for the simulated spot ($r = 0.5$\,mm, gap 4.7\,mm) is $I_{\mathrm{CL}} \propto V^{3/2}/d^2$, equal to 0.08, 0.24 and 0.43\,mA at 100, 200 and 300\,V; the real gun emits 1.46 times that. Maximum thrust therefore scales as $I_{\mathrm{CL}}\sqrt{V} \propto V^2$, and at 100\,V the spot sources about 0.12\,mA whatever the plasma does. In the $\varphi \ll V$ limit the best operating point is the lowest voltage whose emission ceiling still meets the thrust demand; with the float included, the minimum-power voltage sits somewhat higher (Sec.~\ref{sec:mission}). A shorter gap or a wider spot moves the ceiling without changing the voltage.

\subsection{Current collection}
The body collects the one-sided thermal electron flux \cite{san93}
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{th}} = e\,A\,n_e\sqrt{\frac{k T_e}{2\pi m_e}},
\label{eq:ith}
\end{equation}
enhanced by the positive potential. With $\chi = e\varphi/kT_e$,
\begin{equation}
I = \beta\,I_{\mathrm{th}}\,(1+\chi)^{\alpha},
\label{eq:oml}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha = 1$ for a sphere and $0.5$ for a long cylinder in orbital-motion-limited theory, and $\beta$ is a geometry factor. Setting $I$ equal to the escaping current and inverting gives the float. Its stiffness goes as $1/(n_e\sqrt{T_e})$, so thin night-side plasma makes the body float higher for the same current.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{imgs/sheath_regimes.png}
\caption{Thin sheath ($r_p \gg \lambda_D$): electrons reach the body on nearly straight paths and collection scales with surface area. Thick sheath ($r_p \lesssim \lambda_D$): the potential extends far beyond the body and bends electron orbits inward, giving the $(1+\chi)^\alpha$ enhancement of Eq.~\eqref{eq:oml}. The 10\,mm body sits in the thick-sheath regime; a CubeSat would sit in the thin-sheath regime.}
\label{fig:sheath}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Scale}
Drag is proportional to the drag area $S_{\mathrm{ref}}$: the ram face plus free-molecular friction on the walls parallel to the flow, whose coefficient is about 3\,\% of the ram one per unit area but which adds 37\,\% on an end-on body with $L/r = 6$. Collection and body-mounted power are proportional to the skin area. Thrust demand, beam current and power all scale with $S_{\mathrm{ref}}$, so power per unit drag area depends only on altitude and voltage. Vehicle size drops out and what remains is the shape ratio $A_{\mathrm{skin}}/S_{\mathrm{ref}}$. A 10\,mm can and a 3U CubeSat with the same shape have the same feasibility margin. Mass does not enter the demand, since holding altitude takes thrust equal to the drag force; it sets only how fast a craft sinks while thrust falls short, and at equal density a 3U sinks ten times more slowly than the 10\,mm can for the same shortfall.

\subsection{Control}
$I$ and $\varphi$ are plain electrical measurements, so Eqs.~\eqref{eq:thrust}--\eqref{eq:ke} let the spacecraft compute its own thrust in flight. Density, temperature and day/night all collapse into where the body floats, so a two-line servo on the measured float is the whole control law. A microcontroller is sufficient.

\section{PIC simulations}
\label{sec:sims}

\subsection{Setup}
The ladder and characterization runs use WarpX \cite{warpx22} (built on AMReX \cite{amrex19}) in RZ electrostatic mode with the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:setup}; the transverse-field campaign of Sec.~\ref{sec:char} uses the same code in Cartesian 3D. The body is a closed can with a cathode disk inside and an aperture in the lid, modelled as an embedded conductor (Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard}). Its potential is not prescribed. Each step the code counts the charge that left through the aperture and the charge that landed on the skin, and moves the body potential by the net charge over the body's self-capacitance, which is measured once from Gauss's law on a vacuum Laplace solve. The float is therefore an output, and ladder stage 7 checks the method on a bare body against the theoretical floating potential. Ions are repelled by the positive body, so the balance is between the escaping beam and collected electrons only; the reduced ion mass affects how fast the sheath settles, not where it settles. The ambient plasma is a dayside 400\,km row kept constant by a particle reservoir. Every run freezes its configuration and passes fixed acceptance gates on escape fraction, floating potential, current balance and momentum before it is used.

\subsection{Validation ladder}
A PIC simulation of the full device has too many parameters to validate at once. The ladder isolates each piece of physics and checks it against theory before combining them, so the full thruster result inherits confidence from every stage below it. Nine stages build from a vacuum electron gun to the full device (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). All nine pass.

Stages 1--3 test the gun alone in vacuum. Stage 1 confirms that a negative cathode emits and accelerates electrons through a near-neutral aperture, the basic electrode topology, with a 35\,\textmu V energy error on 100\,V; the same topology is confirmed on hardware in Sec.~\ref{sec:bench}. Stage 2 measures aperture transmission at three hole sizes ($\kappa = 0.97$, 0.90 and 1.00, matching the predicted values). Stage 3 confirms transmission is independent of voltage (0.006\,pp spread over 200--300\,V). The transmissions in stages 2--3 are for lightly loaded vacuum guns; the device gun runs at 1.46 times its space-charge scale, which is where the lower $\kappa = 0.81$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:envelope} comes from.

Stages 4--7 test collection on a conducting body in the plasma. Stage 4 reproduces the thermal electron flux of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ith} to within 1\,\%. Stages 5--6 bias the body to $+3$ and $+10$\,V and measure 0.85 and 0.81 of the Laframboise OML ceiling, with the sheath growing from 4.1 to 6.9\,mm as the potential rises. Stage 7 lets the body float freely and measures $-0.251$\,V, inside the bracket between two theoretical models. Stage 8 places two prescribed potentials on one embedded conductor and recovers the exact Laplace solution with zero violation, which validates the multi-potential method used in stage 9. Stage 9 is the full thruster, described next.

Two constants fit all four points:
\begin{align}
F\,[\mathrm{nN}] &= 3.27\; I\,[\mathrm{mA}]\sqrt{\mathrm{KE}\,[\mathrm{eV}]}, \\
\mathrm{KE} &= 0.81\,(V-\varphi).
\end{align}
The thrust slope is 97\,\% of Eq.~\eqref{eq:thrust}; the deficit is plume divergence. $\kappa = 0.81$ is the space-charge depression of a single-aperture gun at this loading. Thrust per watt follows $1/\sqrt{V}$ within 2.5\,\% over 100--300\,V, and the 350\,V point sits 3\,\% below it because the float tax has grown to 14\,\%. The energy efficiency falls slowly from 0.74 to 0.68 as the float tax grows; almost every electron leaves at nearly full energy.

The 300\,V run also selects the collection exponent. Three values of $\alpha$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:oml} agree at 200\,V and predict 31, 36 and 90\,V for the 300\,V float; the measurement is 36.3\,V. A fit across the envelope gives $\alpha = 0.82$--0.89, between the sphere and cylinder limits. The 350\,V float was predicted from that fit at 47\,V and measured at 48.3\,V. One caveat applies to both high-voltage points: the sheath settles on the ion timescale, and their floats were still rising slowly at run end. The quoted values are tail averages; extrapolating the decaying slope puts the settled 300\,V float at 42--48\,V and the settled 350\,V float past 50\,V. No float value is a design limit; the float is a tax on the drive through Eq.~\eqref{eq:ke}, 14\,\% of the supply for the compact body at 350\,V against 4\,\% for the slender body, which is why the slender body matters at 400\,km.

\subsubsection{Shape, density and field}

\paragraph{Shape.} Does elongation hurt current collection? Stretching the can to $L/r = 6$ multiplies the skin by 3.5 at the same ram area. Two hypotheses were recorded before the run: area-only scaling predicted 4--5\,V, and the long-cylinder limit predicted tens of volts. The float dropped to 4.4\,V and the thrust rose 4\,\%, confirming area-only scaling. The pre-registered bracket used a $3.2\times$ area ratio in error; with the correct $3.5\times$ it is 3.8--4.3\,V, 2\,\% below the measurement, so collection grows slightly slower than area. Fitted to both slender runs (200 and 350\,V) at the shared exponent, the slender body collects like 3.1 times the compact body's effective area; the mission model uses that measured value. A 3U CubeSat flying end-on has nearly the same shape ratio (Sec.~\ref{sec:scale}).

\paragraph{Density.} Does thin night-side plasma break the device? At one third density the run was extended to 2.4\,\textmu s (three times the RC time) and the float settled at 42.5\,V. Every fixed-exponent prediction from Eq.~\eqref{eq:oml} overshot, from 53\,V at $\alpha = 1$ to 68\,V at $\alpha = 0.82$. The fitted law overestimates the float in thin plasma, so night-side predictions from Sec.~\ref{sec:mission} are conservative.

\paragraph{Magnetic field.} Does the geomagnetic field tax collection? Along the thrust axis, no. An axial field at flight strength (30\,\textmu T) leaves float, thrust and escape within 0.2\,V, 0.1\,\% and 0.06 percentage points of the reference run; at ten times flight strength collection is taxed, the float rises 33\,V, and the thrust law absorbs the change once the measured float is inserted. The transverse orientation, the one the mission flies in, was then measured on a Cartesian 3D deck: the same body resolved at 1\,mm cells in a $\pm$60\,mm box, the escaped beam prescribed at the lid with the anchor's measured energy, and the thrust ledger corrected for the Lorentz force on the particles in flight, since the exit flux alone under-reads the emission reaction once the beam curls. Runs of 6\,\textmu s settle the float on the model's ion clock; the 800\,ns snapshots reach about half the settled value, and thrust moves under 4\,\%. At flight strength the operating point survives: thrust within 0.8\,\%, escape unchanged, settled float 29.4\,V against the control's 26.8\,V, a 2.6\,V tax, about 1\,\% of the drive. At ten times flight strength the circuit does not close: the float climbs through the 150\,V abort ceiling with no equilibrium below 91\,\% of the beam energy, so in amplified fields the device must fire along the field, the mode the axial runs validate.

\paragraph{400\,km operating point.} Does the slender body at 350\,V close the 400\,km mean drag (32.9\,nN)? The voltage and shape laws together predicted 11--17\,V and 42--43\,nN. It measured 14.0\,V and 43.33\,nN, covering the mean demand with a float well inside the 50\,V limit. The two laws compose, which turns two one-dimensional curves into an operating map over 100--350\,V and $L/r = 0.6$--6.
\section{Mission assessment}
\label{sec:mission}

\subsection{Method}
Drag demand comes from TudatPy \cite{tudat} propagation with NRLMSISE-00 density driven by measured F10.7 and Ap, and IRI-2020 plasma along the orbit. The test body is the slender can of the shape runs (10\,mm diameter, 30\,mm long, $L/r = 6$), flown axial: the thrust axis points along the velocity, which for $L > \tfrac{\pi}{2}r$ is also the low-drag pose. Its drag area $S_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the ram cap plus free-molecular friction on the side wall, with $C_d = 2.2$. Its mass, 3.1\,g, is the density of a 4\,kg 3U; mass does not enter the thrust demand. Circular orbits at 400--700\,km on three families (near-equatorial, $i = 0.5^\circ$; ISS, $51.6^\circ$; sun-synchronous with a 10:30 ascending node) are flown for a year near solar maximum (2024) and minimum (2019). In station keeping the drag is cancelled exactly, so the drag at each 5-minute point is the thrust demand. In free fall the thruster is off and the orbit decays to re-entry (120\,km) or a 5-year cap. The mission model uses the slender body's own collection prefactor (Sec.~\ref{sec:char}). The PIC plasma row of Table~\ref{tab:setup} sits near the dayside top of the 400\,km range; night-side and high-latitude densities are 5--40 times lower, which is where collection is weakest.

Without the thruster the 3\,g body re-enters from 400\,km in 41--46 days and from 550\,km within a year near solar maximum (Table~\ref{tab:cases}, Fig.~\ref{fig:missions}a). With it, the voltage-following model holds 500--700\,km all year for 2.5--48\,mW of mean beam power at solar maximum and 0.1--2\,mW at minimum, with at least 97.5\,\% of the orbit points inside the tested 100--350\,V envelope (Table~\ref{tab:follow}, Fig.~\ref{fig:missions}b). Replaying each year with thrust capped at the measured capability at 350\,V, firing at full capability whenever the craft is below its target and integrating any shortfall into an altitude deficit ($\dot a = 2aF/mv$), the orbit never drops more than 10\,m below its target at 500--700\,km. Solar activity moves the demand 15--25 times; inclination moves it about 10\,\%, partly because an inclined circular orbit flies 10--15\,km higher on average over the oblate Earth. The high-latitude orbits see thinner plasma and float higher, which costs supply voltage at those points, not closure: no voltage or float value is a design limit in this model, and every point of both years has an operating point. At 400\,km the solar-maximum peaks (119--128\,nN) exceed what 350\,V delivers even on average, so that case is not held inside the simulated envelope; at solar minimum 400\,km is held for 21\,mW. Two parts of Table~\ref{tab:follow} are extrapolations, flagged per point in the model output: the collection law below about a third of the simulated density, where the one off-density run found it conservative, and the calibration on 800\,ns floats (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Power}
Power is the thruster's only interface to the spacecraft. Body-mounted cells on the slender can supply about 37\,mW orbit-averaged (30\,\% cells on a third of the skin, 25\,\% illumination; an example, not part of the thruster). Replayed within that budget, the orbit holds at 550--700\,km near solar maximum, with dips up to 1.2\,km at 550\,km, and at every altitude near solar minimum; at 500\,km near maximum the 46--48\,mW demand exceeds it. Supply and demand both grow with area, so the ratio holds with size.

\subsection{Scaling to CubeSats}
\label{sec:scale}
By the argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}, a 3U CubeSat flying end-on ($10\times10\times30$\,cm) has nearly the shape ratio of the slender can and the same feasibility margin: against body-mounted cells it closes 1.1, 2.2 and 4.2 times over at 500, 550 and 600\,km near solar maximum. In absolute terms it needs about 4--5\,W at 500\,km, 2\,W at 550\,km, 1\,W at 600\,km and 0.3\,W at 700\,km near solar maximum, and 0.2\,W, 80\,mW, 40\,mW and 13\,mW near minimum. That puts a 3U in the watt class where electrospray and FEEP already fly (Sec.~\ref{sec:compare}), and above 500\,km its free-fall lifetime already exceeds five years (Table~\ref{tab:cases}); for a CubeSat the device is a propellantless option for altitude control, while for a chip-scale body it decides whether the mission survives. Mass does not change these powers: a 4\,kg 3U sinks about 50\,m/day at 500\,km with the thruster off, against 0.5\,km/day for the 3\,g can, so it tolerates shortfalls far better. Larger bodies also need less collection enhancement over the thermal flux (5.6 times for a 3U at 600\,km against 15 times for the compact body), so they rest on less extrapolation, not more. These numbers are estimates from the fitted collection law, and CubeSat radii sit at 25--60 Debye lengths where that law has not been measured; a thin-sheath estimate puts the 3U float near 60\,V at 500\,km near solar maximum, which would drop that margin below one (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

The thrust per watt is about 200 times below an ion thruster, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:thrust} and cannot be engineered away. At micronewtons the power penalty reaches watts and electrospray or FEEP wins. The difference from every flown system is the hardware column: no tank, no feed system, no neutralizer, no total-impulse limit. Nothing in the table produces thrust below 100\,nN or fits a gram-scale spacecraft, and that is where the device holds altitude on milliwatts.

Related concepts share the return-circuit idea. Bare electrodynamic tethers \cite{san93,kat95} and Janhunen's electric sail and plasma brake \cite{jan04,jan10} all use continuous electron emission with the ambient plasma closing the circuit, but produce thrust by Lorentz force or ion deflection on kilometre-scale tethers. Atmosphere-breathing electric propulsion \cite{zhe20} collects ambient gas but keeps an ionization stage, an acceleration stage and a neutralizer. A recent review of propellantless propulsion \cite{kez25} does not list a standalone ionospheric electron-exchange thruster.

\section{Bench experiment}
\label{sec:bench}
The electrode topology was tested in a laboratory vacuum chamber. A W5W automotive bulb filament with the glass removed, held at $-50$ and $-62$\,V on its two terminals, faces a grounded 4$\times$4\,cm copper plate in a glass chamber at 4--5\,Pa. The ammeter between plate and earth reads a steady 87\,mA once the filament is hot: a single net-negative electrode emits electrons and accelerates them across a gap onto a grounded collector. At this pressure the gap is collisional, so the current includes gas amplification and the result is qualitative. The experiment confirms the principle tested numerically in ladder stage 1: a negative cathode emits and accelerates electrons toward a grounded plate. Controls (bias off, filament cold) and an I--V sweep are the next step.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
Ordered by how much each could change the answer.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Ion clock and reduced ion mass.} Ions are $400\,m_e$. The 3D field campaign settled its floats at 6\,\textmu s on that clock and found the 800\,ns snapshots at about half the settled float, with thrust moving under 2\,\%; real O$^+$ is 73 times heavier still and settles roughly $9\times$ slower, which no run has reached.
  \item \textbf{Prescribed beam.} Beam current is a fixed input, not self-consistently emitted from a modelled cathode. The choice of flight emitter is outside the scope of this paper.
  \item \textbf{CubeSat sheath regime.} Runs sit at $r/\lambda_D \approx 2.5$; CubeSats are 25--60\,$\lambda_D$ across, where orbital-motion-limited collection no longer applies. A single large-body run would settle this.
  \item \textbf{Stationary plasma, held attitude.} No ram flow is modelled, and the mission cases assume a fixed pose without an attitude-control model.
  \item \textbf{Orbit model.} Orbits are circular. Free-fall runs starting in 2024 use forecast solar indices after mid-2025; the 2019 runs are observed throughout. The altitude-hold replay ignores the denser air a dipping craft meets, which is negligible for the metre-scale dips reported and is flagged where a case does not hold.
\end{enumerate}
All powers are beam power $VI$; converter and cathode overheads belong to the flight design.

\section{Conclusion}
A cathode, a high-voltage supply and the spacecraft's own skin make a thruster that needs no propellant. PIC simulations measure its operating envelope at 3--43\,nN from 12--278\,mW, with two constants describing every run to within 3\,\%, a floating potential fixed by a measured collection law, and a return current that costs 1--9\,\% of the beam thrust. Against a year of real drag on three orbit families at both ends of the solar cycle, with the float included, a 3\,g slender body that re-enters within a year from 550\,km without the thruster holds 500--700\,km with it for 2.5--48\,mW of beam power near solar maximum and 0.1--2\,mW near minimum, within its own body-mounted solar supply from 550\,km up. The feasibility condition depends on shape and altitude but not on size, so a 3U CubeSat inherits it at about 1\,W at 600\,km near solar maximum and tens of milliwatts near minimum. The device is cheap to test: it needs no hardware beyond a supply and an electron gun of the kind that already flies as a neutralizer, and it can be retrofitted to an existing ion or Hall thruster after its propellant is spent. The transverse geomagnetic field, once the largest open question, is now measured: at flight strength it leaves the operating point unchanged, and at ten times flight strength it forces field-aligned firing. The flight emitter is the next work.
