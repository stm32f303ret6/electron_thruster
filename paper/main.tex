\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Atmospheric drag can limit the orbital lifetime of small spacecraft, while their size and power budgets constrain propulsion hardware. This paper investigates an electron thruster that uses the ionosphere to supply its return current, eliminating the need for stored propellant. A cathode biased below the spacecraft body emits an electron beam through an aperture; the body charges positively and collects ambient electrons to replenish the emitted charge. Particle-in-cell simulations (WarpX) of conducting bodies 10\,mm in diameter predict 3.4-43\,nN of beam thrust at 100-350\,V, with beam-supply powers of 12-278\,mW and escape fractions of 96-99\,\%. A model calibrated to these simulations and coupled to year-long orbital and ionospheric calculations estimates the power required for drag compensation. For a body 10\,mm in diameter and 30\,mm long in sun-synchronous orbits at 500-600\,km, the estimated mean beam-supply demand is 10-46\,mW near solar maximum and 0.4-2\,mW near solar minimum. These mission estimates extrapolate the collection model beyond the simulated plasma conditions and exclude emitter and power-conversion overheads. The results support further investigation of electron propulsion for small spacecraft; extension to larger bodies requires validation of current-collection scaling and emitter integration.
\end{abstract}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.4cm}

\section{Introduction}

Conventional gridded ion thrusters require stored propellant, a feed system and a neutralizer \cite{nasasoa26}. This hardware adds mass, volume and integration complexity. This paper investigates a simpler architecture: emit electrons and let the ionosphere provide the return current.

The tradeoff is thrust per watt. For a directed exhaust, $F/P = 2\eta/v_e$, where $\eta$ is the electrical-to-jet efficiency and $v_e$ is the exhaust speed. Electrons accelerated through a few hundred volts reach high exhaust speeds, giving little momentum per unit energy. The simulated 200\,V operating point delivers about 0.2\,\textmu N/W, making electron propulsion power-intensive at millinewton thrust levels.

At the nanonewton drag levels examined here, the beam-supply demand is milliwatts. A cathode, an aperture, a high-voltage supply and the spacecraft's conducting skin provide propulsion without stored propellant. The aim is to reduce the hardware needed for station keeping on small spacecraft; emitter and converter overheads remain part of the flight design.

\section{Concept}

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{imgs/concept_steps_mpl.pdf}
\caption{Operating sequence. The supply is switched on, the cathode emits, the escaping beam charges the body positive, and the positive body collects ambient electrons until collection equals emission.}
\label{fig:concept}
\end{figure*}

A cathode held at $-V$ with respect to the spacecraft body emits electrons and accelerates them out through an aperture (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}). Each escaping electron leaves the body one charge more positive, so the body's conducting skin collects ambient electrons from the plasma, and the supply returns them to the cathode. The body settles at the floating potential $\varphi$ where collection equals emission. The beam is not recaptured: its electrons carry 100-300\,eV, while $\varphi$ is a few to a few tens of volts and the ambient electrons carry 0.1\,eV. The collected electrons arrive from all directions and cancel 0.4-1.4\,\% of the beam thrust at 200-350\,V.

\section{Theory}

\begingroup
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{5pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{5pt}
\setlength{\abovedisplayshortskip}{0pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayshortskip}{3pt}
The concept is a plain electrostatic accelerator.

Thrust is the momentum flux of the escaping beam:
\begin{equation}
F = \frac{I_{\mathrm{esc}}\sqrt{2m_e\,\mathrm{KE}}}{e}.
\label{eq:thrust}
\end{equation}
Here $I_{\mathrm{esc}} = \fesc I$ is the escaping part of the emitted current; collimated exhaust is assumed.

The energy each electron actually leaves with is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KE} = \kappa\,e\,(V-\varphi),
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with KE in joules and $V$ the supply voltage.

Jet power divided by electrical beam-supply power is
\begin{equation}
\eta = \frac{P_{\mathrm{jet}}}{P}
     = \kappa\,\frac{V-\varphi}{V}\,\fesc.
\label{eq:efficiency}
\end{equation}
Here $P = VI$; emitter and converter overheads are excluded.

The energy fraction $\kappa$ reflects the simulated launch position; emission at cathode potential ideally gives $\kappa \approx 1$. The float potential $\varphi$ depends on collecting area, body shape and plasma density. The ``float tax'' $\varphi/V$ is the fraction of supply voltage lost to current return. The escape fraction $\fesc$ is the beam fraction that clears the body, controlled by the exit-aperture geometry.

\noindent\textbf{Control.} Electrical measurements of current and floating potential can provide a calibrated thrust estimate. Feedback on the float can account for plasma density, temperature and day/night changes without an explicit ionosphere model.
\par\endgroup

\section{Simulated capability}

The device is simulated with WarpX \cite{warpx22,amrex19} in RZ electrostatic mode: a closed 10\,mm can with a cathode disk inside and an aperture in the lid, in a dayside 400\,km plasma ($n_e = 1.63\times10^{12}$\,m$^{-3}$, $kT_e = 114$\,meV, ions at $400\,m_e$), 0.15\,mm cells, 800\,ns runs. The body potential is not prescribed: each step it moves by the net charge that left and landed over the body's self-capacitance. A ladder of nine stages builds from a vacuum electron gun to the full device, checking each piece against theory (thermal collection within 1\,\%); all pass, and every run passes fixed gates on escape, current balance and momentum. Table~\ref{tab:envelope} gives the operating envelope. Two constants describe it: $F = 3.27\,I\sqrt{\mathrm{KE}}$ (with $F$ in nN, $I$ in mA and KE in eV; the slope includes interception and divergence) and $\kappa = 0.81$. Stretching the can to 30\,mm ($L/r = 6$) multiplies its skin by 3.5 at the same frontal area: the float drops fourfold and thrust rises. A 3D run with the geomagnetic field across the beam at flight strength changes the thrust by under 1\,\%.

$\kappa = 0.81$ is a property of the simulation, not the device: the beam is launched 0.3\,mm above the cathode, where the potential is already 19\,\% of $V$ above it, and the measured deficit is 17-18\,\% of $V$ in every run. A real cathode emits at cathode potential ($\kappa \approx 1$), so every thrust and power figure here is conservative by about 11\,\%; a confirmation run is under way.

Drag and the plasma along the orbit come from a year-long TudatPy propagation \cite{tudat} with NRLMSISE-00 density \cite{msis} driven by measured solar and geomagnetic indices and IRI-2020 plasma \cite{iri} along the orbit, near solar maximum (2024) and minimum (2019). The test body is the slender can of Table~\ref{tab:envelope}, 3.1\,g, flown with its axis along the velocity, so the thrust axis and the low-drag pose coincide. With the thruster on, drag is cancelled exactly and the drag at each 5-minute point is the thrust demand; the supply voltage follows it at the least beam power, with the float estimated from the local plasma using the simulation-calibrated collection model. With the thruster off, the orbit decays freely.

Table~\ref{tab:mission} gives the result. Without the thruster the 3\,g body re-enters from 400\,km in 46 days and from 550\,km in 11 months near solar maximum. With it, 500-700\,km is held all year: the mean demand of 0.7-9\,nN sits inside the measured envelope, for 2.5-46\,mW near maximum and under 2\,mW near minimum. At 400\,km near maximum the 38\,nN mean is below the 43\,nN measured at 350\,V, but the thin night-side plasma pushes half the points past 350\,V; near minimum 400\,km is held for 21\,mW. The ISS and equatorial orbits differ from the sun-synchronous one by about 10\,\%.

The demand is not steady. It swings about $1.7\times$ over each orbit (day and night) and up to $2.5$-$2.8\times$ on storm days (11 May and 7-11 October 2024). Holding altitude is an impulse budget, so the peaks cost a small dip, not the orbit: replayed with thrust capped at the measured 350\,V capability, the orbit never drops more than 10\,m below its target at 500-700\,km.

Drag, current and beam power all scale with the drag area, so the concept does not depend on size. A 1U CubeSat has 103 times the can's drag area and needs 103 times its thrust and, to first order, its power: about 1\,W at 600\,km near solar maximum and tens of milliwatts near minimum. A 1U lives longer in free fall because its mass grows faster than its area: 167 days from 400\,km near solar maximum, against 46 for the can.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Settling.} Ions are $400\,m_e$ and floats are 800\,ns snapshots; a 3D campaign settled its floats at 6\,\textmu s at about twice the 800\,ns value, with thrust moving by a few percent.
  \item \textbf{Prescribed beam.} Beam current is an input, launched above the cathode ($\kappa$ above); the flight emitter is not chosen.
  \item \textbf{CubeSat regime.} The simulated body is 2.5 Debye lengths across; a 1U is about 25, where the collection model has not been validated.
  \item \textbf{Attitude and plasma flow.} A held pose is assumed and the plasma is stationary (no ram flow).
\end{enumerate}
All powers are beam power $VI$; converter and cathode overheads belong to the flight design.

\section{Conclusion}
A cathode, a high-voltage supply and the spacecraft's own skin make a thruster that needs no propellant. Simulations measure 3.4-43\,nN from 12-278\,mW, enough to hold a 3\,g chip-scale spacecraft at 500-700\,km for milliwatts where it would otherwise re-enter within a year, and the same physics scales with area to CubeSats at around a watt. The next steps are a settled, cathode-launched confirmation run and a large-body run in the CubeSat regime.
