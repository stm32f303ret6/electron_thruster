\begin{abstract}
We describe and simulate a spacecraft thruster with no stored propellant, no
tank, and no neutralizer: a cold electron gun sealed inside a floating
conducting body emits an electron beam through a single aperture; the body
charges positive until it recollects ambient ionospheric electrons at exactly
the emitted rate. For a gram-class ``chipsat'' body (\O10\,mm can) at a
representative dayside ionospheric operating point, pre-registered and
acceptance-gated particle-in-cell simulations (WarpX, RZ electrostatic)
measure the complete thrust--power frontier across the 100--300\,V hardware
range: 3.42\,nN at 12.1\,mW, 13.65\,nN at 68.4\,mW, and 30.13\,nN at
189\,mW, confirming the $F/P \propto 1/\sqrt{V}$ throttle law within 2.5\%,
with beam escape $\ge 96\%$ and a benign floating potential
($\le$36\,V) at every point. The same three runs discriminate between
pre-registered forms of the ambient-electron collection law: the linear and
square-root laws are refuted and the sub-linear form
$I \propto (1+e\varphi/kT_e)^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha \approx 0.82$--0.89
survives, which collapses the optimal throttle policy into a two-line flight
controller servoed on the spacecraft's own measured floating potential -- no
ionosphere model on board. A minimal executable model calibrated only from
the three gated runs, applied to year-long orbit propagations with real
solar-activity and ionosphere data, bounds the mission envelope: drag
compensation closes on body-mounted solar power at 550--600\,km, closes on
impulse (duty-cycled, power-bound) at 500\,km, and does not close at
400\,km. In an independent predecessor implementation of the same mechanism
(\O14\,mm body, 800\,V) a closed control-volume momentum ledger, validated
on an analytic vacuum fixture and a dedicated full-return null experiment,
established that the recollected return current does not cancel the thrust
(closed thrust $= F_{\mathrm{beam}} - 2.3\%$); in the present campaign the
all-species momentum deposited by collected particles is gated per run and
measures 1.0--8.8\% of beam thrust. We present this as a
feasibility-concept study: the device physics is measured and gated, the
mission projection is model-based with every extrapolated regime explicitly
flagged, and flight validation is future work.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Centimetre- to decimetre-class spacecraft in low Earth orbit below
$\sim$600\,km lose altitude to atmospheric drag on timescales of months to a
few years. Extending their operational life requires continuous thrust of a
few to a few tens of nanonewtons, delivered from a power budget of tens to a
few hundred milliwatts -- a regime that sits below where any flight-proven
electric propulsion system is efficient. Cold-gas, PPT, electrospray and FEEP
systems all carry a finite propellant mass in a tank, with feed lines,
valves, or emitter arrays that add mass, cost, and single-point failure modes
disproportionate to a spacecraft this small; gridded-ion and Hall thrusters
are efficient only at powers one to three orders of magnitude above this
budget. The result is a persistent gap: the smallest, cheapest spacecraft
have the least propulsion available to them, at exactly the scale where
station keeping would extend mission life the most cheaply.

We revisit a device long flown in a different role. Every gridded-ion and
Hall thruster carries an electron gun -- the neutralizer -- whose sole job is
to keep the spacecraft from charging as it expels positive ions. We ask what
happens if that same gun, with no ion stage at all, is run as the entire
thruster: fire electrons out through an aperture in an otherwise closed
conducting body, and let the body's own positive charge draw ambient
ionospheric electrons back in to close the circuit. The ionosphere supplies
both the neutralizing return current \emph{and}, in the sense that matters
for a reaction engine, the propellant -- at a mass flow measured in
nanograms per year. No propellant is carried, stored, or metered; the only
consumable is electrical power.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we measure the complete
thrust--power frontier of this mechanism across its full hardware voltage
range (100--300\,V) with pre-registered, acceptance-gated particle-in-cell
simulation at a realistic ionospheric operating point
(Sec.~\ref{sec:frontier}), and show it delivers ion-thruster-class
\emph{energy} conversion ($\eta \approx 0.73$) at a deliberately tiny
\emph{momentum} economy ($F/P \approx 0.16$--0.28\,\textmu N/W) -- the pair
of numbers that explains why the device owns the nanonewton regime and
nothing above it (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}). Second, the three frontier
points discriminate between pre-registered candidate forms of the
ambient-electron collection law (Sec.~\ref{sec:collection}); the surviving
sub-linear law collapses the optimal throttle policy into a two-line
controller servoed on the spacecraft's own floating potential
(Sec.~\ref{sec:throttle}) -- the device measures its environment by
existing. Third, because the returning electron current is the natural
objection to calling this thrust real, we summarize the closed
control-volume momentum ledger and full-return null experiment of the
predecessor implementation and report the per-run momentum gates of the
present campaign (Sec.~\ref{sec:ledger}). Fourth, a minimal executable
model, calibrated only from the gated runs and with every out-of-envelope
prediction explicitly flagged, converts year-long orbit propagations into a
mission feasibility corridor (Sec.~\ref{sec:application}). Throughout we
argue the concept is unusually cheap to test: it needs no new hardware
beyond a high-voltage supply and an existing class of electron gun, and can
retrofit the neutralizer already flying on an ion or Hall thruster
(Sec.~\ref{sec:practical}).

\section{Operating principle}
\label{sec:principle}
The device is a conducting body with a cold-cathode electron gun inside and a
single beam aperture. The full cycle, with measured values from the 200\,V
reference run:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A high-voltage supply holds the cathode at $-V$ relative to the body;
        the cathode is the most negative node in the system, so emitted
        electrons accelerate \emph{away} from it, through the body aperture.
  \item Electrons leave the body at energy $\approx e(V-\varphi)$ minus
        injection-plane space charge (measured mean exhaust energy
        147.5\,eV at $V = 200$\,V; the measured energy fraction
        $\mathrm{KE}/(V-\varphi) = 0.81$ is constant across the voltage
        range).
  \item Each departed electron leaves the body one charge positive; the body
        potential rises (measured float at the reference: $+16.98$\,V).
  \item The positive body attracts ambient ionospheric electrons, which are
        collected on its conducting surfaces; in equilibrium the collected
        current equals the escaped current exactly (measured current balance
        closes to 1.5--3.5\% at all three operating points). The ionosphere
        is both the propellant reservoir and the neutralizer.
  \item The escaping beam carries net momentum; its reaction is thrust. The
        recollected electrons arrive at thermal-plus-$e\varphi$ energies from
        all directions and deposit a small net momentum (measured and gated
        per run, Sec.~\ref{sec:ledger}).
\end{enumerate}
No valves, no flow control, no gimbals, no plasma production stage: thrust is
throttled by the emission current and supply voltage alone.

\section{Simulation method and evidence discipline}
\label{sec:method}
All production runs use WarpX~\cite{warpx22} (v26.5, GPU), RZ electrostatic,
with the spacecraft as an embedded-boundary conductor whose floating
potential is driven each step by the measured charge balance (a
``charge-pump'' update: emitted charge out, collected charge in, body
capacitance from a Gauss-law probe). The chipsat deck: a \O10\,mm
$\times$\,5.9\,mm closed can with a 4\,mm exhaust aperture and an internal
cathode disk; ambient plasma at a dayside 400\,km-class row
($n_e = 1.627\times10^{12}$\,m$^{-3}$, $T_e = 1318.8$\,K,
$kT_e = 113.6$\,meV), maintained by a particle reservoir (infinite-ionosphere
recycle -- without refill a floating equilibrium measures reservoir
depletion, not physics); grid $dr = dz = 0.15$\,mm sized to the Debye length
(1.97\,mm); CFL-derived time step; 16 particles per cell per species;
reduced ion mass ($400\,m_e$, caveat in Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations});
800\,ns simulated per run ($\approx$29 capacitive settle times at the
reference point).

Because this paper's claims rest on a small number of expensive runs, the
campaign enforces an explicit evidence contract. Every run freezes and
hashes its full physics config; an atomic manifest records the config hash,
git commit, random seed, code version, and expected-vs-observed final
iteration; interrupted runs are discarded as evidence, never resumed.
Acceptance gates (escape fraction, benign float, steady current balance,
momentum bound, sheath containment, and two independent ledger-vs-particle-dump
charge cross-checks, which close to $\sim$$10^{-9}$) are fixed in versioned
policy files \emph{before} each run; the scaling-law predictions for the
100\,V and 300\,V points, and the competing collection-law hypotheses of
Sec.~\ref{sec:collection}, were pre-registered in the repository before
those runs completed. Below the production stages sits a validation ladder
run under the same contract: an analytic two-electrode Laplace rung, thermal
collection against one-sided flux theory (closes to $\pm$1\%), biased
collection at two potentials, a bare floating body, and electron-gun
emission rungs. The curated metrics, verdicts, frozen configs, and run
manifests of every result used in this paper are committed alongside the
code.

Two efficiencies describe this device, and the paper keeps them deliberately
side by side. The \emph{energy conversion}
$\eta = P_{\mathrm{jet}}/P_{\mathrm{supply}} \approx 0.73$ at the reference
point -- ion-thruster-class conversion, nearly flat in $V$ -- because the
exhaust is electrons and nearly all of them escape at nearly full energy.
The \emph{impulse economy} $F/P \approx 0.2$\,\textmu N/W at 200\,V,
$\propto 1/\sqrt{V}$ -- roughly 200$\times$ below a gridded ion thruster.
Both are true simultaneously: superb energy-to-jet conversion at tiny
momentum per watt. The second number is why the device only owns the
nanonewton regime; the first is why it deserves to
(Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}).

The frontier confirms the throttle law $P \propto F\sqrt{V}$ at fixed
thrust: every doubling of voltage costs $\sim$40\% more power, so
\emph{slow-and-many beats fast-and-few} on momentum per watt, and the
cheapest feasible operating point is the lowest voltage whose emission
ceiling still meets the thrust demand. The 100\,V floor run measures
exactly that boundary: escape degrades to 96.1\% (the space-charge tax of
low-voltage optics) but clears the 95\% gate -- the power-optimal end of
the throttle curve is feasible, at 0.283\,\textmu N/W.

\section{The collection law: a pre-registered discrimination}
\label{sec:collection}
Every escaping electron must be replaced by a collected ambient one; the
floating potential is whatever makes that balance close. The collection
side is therefore the physics that sets the operating point, and its form
was the campaign's principal open question. Writing the collected current
as $I = \beta A\, j_{\mathrm{the}}(n_e,T_e)\,(1+\chi)^{\alpha}$ with
$\chi = e\varphi/kT_e$ and $j_{\mathrm{the}}$ the one-sided thermal flux,
three candidate exponents existed in the project lineage, all agreeing at
the 200\,V anchor ($\chi \approx 150$) and diverging at the 300\,V point
($\chi \approx 320$). Their predictions for the 300\,V float were recorded
in the repository before the run: $\alpha = 1$ (linear, orbital-motion
style) predicts $+31$\,V; $\alpha = 0.82 \pm 0.06$ (fitted across six
equilibria of the predecessor campaign) predicts $+36$\,V; $\alpha = 0.5$
predicts $+90$\,V, which would fail the benign-float gate.

The measured 300\,V float is $+36.30$\,V (tail-averaged under the
pre-registered analysis policy): the linear law is refuted (the float
passes 31\,V and keeps climbing), the square-root law is refuted (nothing
in the trajectory approaches 90\,V), and $\alpha = 0.82$ survives
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}, centre). One caveat was pre-registered with the
test and applies in full: at high $\chi$ the sheath equilibrates on the ion
timescale, and the run's late slope, though decaying ($+44 \to +27$\,mV/ns
over the final 200\,ns), had not reached zero; extrapolating the decay
places the settled float at $\approx$42--48\,V, brushing but not breaking
the 50\,V design limit. A least-squares fit of $\alpha$ across all three
measured points gives $\alpha = 0.89$ (tail-averaged floats) to
$\alpha = 0.85$ (settled-extrapolated floats), bracketing the upper edge of
the pre-registered band. The float robs the beam -- the accelerating
potential is always $V - \varphi$ -- so this law is also the charging tax
schedule: $\varphi$ eats 5\% of the supply at 100\,V and 12\% at 300\,V.

\section{The throttle rule: a two-line servo on the float}
\label{sec:throttle}
The predecessor campaign measured what happens at \emph{fixed} thrust as
voltage drops (five converged runs, 78--300\,V): the required current
rises, the charging tax and escape collapse explode below $\sim$100\,V, and
specific power $P/F$ traces a U with a shallow valley. The valley has a
closed form from the marginal-cost balance of the collection law:
$V_{\mathrm{opt}} = ((2\alpha+1)/\alpha)\,\varphi_{\mathrm{eq}} =
3.1$--$3.2\,\varphi_{\mathrm{eq}}$ for the measured $\alpha$. Because
$\varphi_{\mathrm{eq}}$ already encodes the plasma density, temperature,
day/night state, and the vehicle's own current draw, the optimal controller
needs no ionosphere model and no lookup table:
\begin{quote}
\begin{tabular}{l}
1.\quad $V \approx 3.1\,\varphi$ \hfill (servo the supply to the measured float)\\
2.\quad $I = F_{\mathrm{req}} / \big(c_F\sqrt{\kappa(V-\varphi)}\big)$
   \hfill (current from the thrust demand)\\
\quad\ guards: $I \le 1.46\,I_{\mathrm{CL}}(V)$;\quad $\varphi \le 50$\,V;\quad
   infeasible $\Rightarrow$ duty-cycle at the valley.\\
\end{tabular}
\end{quote}
The spacecraft measures its environment by existing: a different orbit,
altitude, or solar cycle produces different $\varphi$ readings and the
servo follows. The constants ($c_F = 3.27$\,nN/(mA$\sqrt{\mathrm{eV}}$),
$\kappa = 0.81$, the 1.46 emission ratio, $\alpha$) are measured, not
tuned, and the valley is shallow (flat over $\sim$110--160\,V at the
dayside row), so a 20\% error in the servo factor costs a few percent in
power. Simulations under this rule are not a lookup table: they measure the
few constants, validate the rule's form at stress points, and map the guard
edges.

\section{The momentum ledger: is the thrust real?}
\label{sec:ledger}
The first question a plasma physicist asks of this concept: the emitted
current \emph{returns} -- does its momentum cancel the thrust? The answer is
measured at two levels.

In the predecessor implementation (\O14\,mm body, 800\,V, 17.1\,nN at
174\,mW) we closed the full control-volume momentum balance every
simulation window: per-species momentum flux through every boundary,
deposition on the body, emission reaction, field terms
($\int\rho E_z\,dV$, Maxwell stress), and the stored-momentum transient.
Two validation fixtures passed pre-registered gates: a \textbf{vacuum
escape} case with an analytic answer (residual $+0.80$\,nN against a 1\,nN
gate), and a \textbf{full-return null} in which the body is pinned above
the beam energy so escape is 0.0\% -- the impact channel alone reads
$-12.1$\,nN, and the closed thrust is $-0.39\,\text{nN} \approx 0$:
emission recoil, field pull and impact cancel exactly as momentum
conservation demands. At the operating point the ledger closes at
$F = F_{\mathrm{beam}} - 2.3\%$; notably, the return-current impact channel
is cancelled by the field pull on the body, so the na\"ive subtraction
$F_{\mathrm{beam}} - F_{\mathrm{impact}}$ \emph{underestimates} thrust.
\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figs/ledger_diagram.pdf}
\caption{Momentum-ledger control volume at the predecessor operating point
(\O14\,mm, 800\,V). Solid arrows are directly measured particle momentum
flux; the dashed arrow is the field force on the craft. The impact channel
is almost entirely cancelled by the field pull, not by the escaping beam --
the two together leave the closed thrust within 2.3\% of
$F_{\mathrm{beam}}$ alone.}
\label{fig:ledger}
\end{figure}

In the present campaign the same question is gated on every production run:
the ledger separates $F_{\mathrm{beam}}$ (escaping-beam reaction) from
$F_{\mathrm{net}}$, the $z$-momentum deposited by \emph{all} species
landing on the craft -- collected ambient electrons and ions plus the
returning beam fraction -- and the acceptance policy bounds their ratio.
Measured: $|F_{\mathrm{net}}|/F_{\mathrm{beam}} = 1.0\%$ at 300\,V
(0.30\,nN against 30.13) and 8.8\% at 100\,V, where the slower beam and
lower escape make the return channel relatively largest. The physics
ceiling is worth stating: at current balance the collected current equals
the escaped current, but collected electrons arrive with
$\sim e\varphi$ of directed energy against the beam's
$e\,\mathrm{KE}$, so even if every collected electron arrived from directly
astern the cancellation could not exceed
$\sqrt{\varphi/\mathrm{KE}} \approx 41\%$ (300\,V values); isotropic
arrival -- electrons fall onto the body from all directions -- reduces it
to the measured percent level.

\section{Numerical credibility}
\label{sec:credibility}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Code-to-code trajectory validation}: the present (migrated,
        GPU) implementation reproduces the predecessor implementation's
        converged 200\,V anchor point-by-point: float to 0.05\%, escape
        fraction to 0.001\%, $F_{\mathrm{beam}}$ to 0.09\% over 380 logged
        windows.
  \item \textbf{Collection physics vs.\ theory}: the validation ladder's
        thermal-collection rung closes against one-sided flux theory to
        $\pm$1\%; biased rungs measure the finite-size collection
        enhancement at $\chi = 26$ and 88 before any thruster stage runs.
  \item \textbf{Ledger-vs-dump cross-checks}: the per-step scrape ledger is
        checked against independently written particle dumps for both the
        ambient-electron and beam-escape channels on every gated run;
        agreement is at the $10^{-9}$ level (gate: $2\times10^{-2}$).
  \item \textbf{Determinism}: runs are seeded and reproduce their physics
        under rerun; every number in this paper carries its run manifest
        (config hash, seed, code version, git commit).
  \item \textbf{Convergence}: in the predecessor implementation, time step
        ($\times$2 finer), particles-per-cell ($\times$2), and RNG seed each
        change every reported quantity by $\le$0.5\%; grid refinement from
        0.30 to 0.20\,mm cells shifted thrust $+4$--5\% and exhaust energy
        $+10\%$, reported there as the leading open numerical question. For
        the present chipsat deck (0.15\,mm cells -- already finer than
        both predecessor grids) we ran a two-axis convergence pair at the
        200\,V anchor. Doubling particles-per-cell (ambient and beam)
        changes every reported quantity by $\le$0.05\% over the full
        800\,ns record, all gates PASS -- particle statistics are a closed
        axis. Grid refinement ($dx\,0.15\!\to\!0.10$\,mm, CFL-locked
        $\Delta t$) shifts $F_{\mathrm{beam}}$ $+4.0$\%, exhaust KE
        $+7.4$\%, and $\varphi$ $-1.8$\%, compared over a matched
        430--536\,ns window (the fine-grid run's step cap ends it at
        536\,ns; the comparison uses the identical physical window on both
        runs, the same fixed-protocol approach as the predecessor's study).
        Grid resolution therefore remains the leading numerical
        uncertainty; we carry it as the error band on
        Table~\ref{tab:frontier} ($F\ {\pm}4$\%, KE ${\pm}7$\%,
        $\varphi\ {\pm}2$\%) and note its sign is conservative for every
        gate: finer grids give \emph{more} thrust and a \emph{lower}
        float. A third grid point to establish the asymptotic order is
        future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Comparison with existing micropropulsion}
\label{sec:comparison}

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
The idea that a spacecraft can dispense with stored propellant by exchanging
charge with its ambient plasma environment has deep roots, though not, to our
knowledge, in exactly the form proposed here. The theoretical foundation for
our device's collection physics is the bare-tether electron-collection theory
of Sanmart\'in, Mart\'inez-S\'anchez, and Ahedo~\cite{san93}, who showed that
an uninsulated conductor floating positive relative to the ambient ionosphere
collects ambient electrons in the orbital-motion-limited (OML) regime -- the
regime whose sub-linear finite-body form our frontier runs measure directly
(Sec.~\ref{sec:collection}); collection by a positively-biased tether under
a \emph{flowing} (ram/drift), rather than stationary, LEO plasma was
subsequently measured experimentally by Siguier et al.~\cite{sig13}, directly
relevant to the ram-wake limitation flagged in Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}.
This theory was flown and confirmed by NASA's Plasma Motor Generator (PMG)
tether experiment in 1993~\cite{pmg93,kat95}, which demonstrated
hollow-cathode plasma contactors providing a low-impedance
electron-emission-and-collection connection to the ionosphere at each end of
a 500-m tether -- a two-terminal flown precedent for the single-body electron
exchange our device performs internally between its beam aperture and its
own conducting surface. Closer in spirit to a standalone propellantless
thruster is Janhunen's electric solar wind sail
(E-sail)~\cite{jan04}, in which a continuously operating electron gun holds a
tether at high positive potential, actuating tether voltage by ``pump[ing]
out negative charge from the system''~\cite{jan14}, so that it electrostatically
deflects ambient solar-wind protons and converts that deflection into thrust
by momentum conservation; Janhunen separately proposed a LEO-ionospheric
variant, the electrostatic ``plasma brake''~\cite{jan10,jan09neg}, which uses a
tether of the \emph{opposite}, negative polarity to produce Coulomb drag
against the ionospheric ram flow for deorbiting, rather than the positive
polarity of the E-sail or of our device's body. Our device differs from both
in its thrust mechanism -- direct recoil from an escaping electron beam
recollected on a closed body, rather than electrostatic deflection of
ambient ions around a charged tether of either polarity -- but shares their
defining feature that continuous electron emission, not stored propellant,
is what makes the mechanism propellantless. An electrodynamic tether is
also the natural efficiency benchmark: per watt, Lorentz-force thrust on a
kilometre-scale tether far exceeds any reaction engine, ours included. The
comparison inverts at chipsat scale: a gram-class vehicle cannot deploy,
stabilize, or survive a conductor $10^{4}$--$10^{6}$ times its own length,
tether thrust is constrained to the $I\mathbf{L}\times\mathbf{B}$
direction, and a bare tether still requires electron collection and
emission at its terminations -- the same contactor physics this device
\emph{is}, minus the tether. Zubrin's dipole
drive~\cite{zub17} similarly treats ambient space plasma itself as reaction
mass, accelerating collected ions between charged screens while rejecting
electrons, though it targets magnetospheric and interstellar-precursor
regimes rather than low-Earth-orbit drag makeup. A body of ``ambient
atmosphere ion thruster'' patent literature~\cite{dre07} and the broader field
of Atmosphere-Breathing Electric Propulsion (ABEP)~\cite{zhe20} use similar
language -- collecting ambient constituents in place of a propellant tank --
but a materially different mechanism: they collect neutral atmosphere or ram
ions and ionize/accelerate them with a conventional electric-propulsion
stage, retaining an ionization stage, an acceleration stage, and a separate
neutralizer; our device requires none of these, since the ionosphere supplies
its reaction mass pre-ionized and its own neutralizing return current through
the same aperture that emits the beam. A recent general review of
propellantless propulsion concepts~\cite{kez25}, covering gravity assists,
solar/magnetic/electric sails, and quantum-vacuum thrust, does not treat
ambient-ionospheric-electron-exchange thrusters as a category, suggesting the
specific mechanism proposed here -- a sealed body, a cold electron gun, and
nothing else -- has not previously been proposed or evaluated as a standalone
low-Earth-orbit micropropulsion device.

\subsection{Quantitative comparison}
\label{sec:comparison-table}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} positions our device against seven representative,
flight-proven or flight-qualified micropropulsion systems spanning cold gas
through Hall-effect propulsion, plus a passive photon-pressure baseline, each
sourced from a manufacturer datasheet, a peer-reviewed paper, or NASA's 2026
\emph{State of the Art of Small Spacecraft Technology} report~\cite{nasasoa26}.
The comparison makes three points. First, every incumbent's \emph{lowest
flight-representative operating power} is at least an order of magnitude
above even our 300\,V ceiling point (189\,mW) -- FEEP, the lowest-power
flight electric propulsion technology surveyed, states explicitly that ``the
required power to operate the IFM starts at around 8\,W''~\cite{enpifm} --
and no system in the table is characterized, or apparently characterizable,
at our thrust and power scale. Second, where an explicit or computable jet
efficiency exists, it clusters at 28--45\% for
electrospray~\cite{busekbetmax21}, $\approx$35\% for FEEP~\cite{krejci19},
and $\approx$31--44\% for Hall thrusters~\cite{busekbht200}; our measured
energy conversion (68\% at the predecessor point, 73\% at the chipsat
reference) exceeds every value found. Third -- and this is a boundary, not
a boast -- our thrust-per-watt is $\sim$200$\times$ below gridded ion: the
efficiency-indifference boundary is real. The $F/P$ penalty is invisible at
nanonewtons (milliwatts), noticeable at micronewtons ($\sim$10\,W for
CubeSat drag -- disqualifying), and decisive at millinewtons. The natural
handoff -- this device below $\sim$0.1\,\textmu N, electrospray at
micronewtons, ion/Hall at millinewtons -- is part of the claim, not a
concession. The smallest controllable flight EP (precision
electrosprays/FEEP, $\sim$5--30\,\textmu N with $\sim$0.1\,\textmu N
resolution~\cite{nasasoa26}) sits two to three orders of magnitude above
chipsat drag, in kilogram-class packages; ``continuous'' thrust at
nanonewtons is not a propellant-mass argument (five years of nanonewton
thrust is $\sim$0.3\,g of electrospray propellant) but a \emph{dry-system
floor} argument: tank + feed + valves + PPU is $\sim$0.3--1\,kg and does
not shrink to gram scale, while this concept's floor is a cathode, a boost
converter, and the spacecraft's own skin.

\section{Application: the mission feasibility corridor}
\label{sec:application}
The three gated runs calibrate a minimal executable model (committed with
the repository) that applies the flight rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:throttle} to
every row of a year-long orbit propagation: per five-minute timestep, the
local plasma density and temperature (IRI-2020) and drag (NRLMSISE-00, real
F10.7/Ap, TudatPy propagation~\cite{tudat}) enter, and the model returns
the servo's operating point $(V, I, \varphi)$, the power, and -- critically
-- a per-row envelope flag: any row whose density, $\chi$, or perveance
falls outside what a committed run has measured is counted as
\emph{extrapolated}, never silently averaged in. Five missions (gram-class
chipsat, 400--600\,km): Table~\ref{tab:missions}, Fig.~\ref{fig:missions}.

The corridor reads directly: at \textbf{550--600\,km} drag compensation
closes on body-mounted solar power (8--17\,mW mean demand against
$\sim$30\,mW harvest) with 25--32\% duty cycles; \textbf{500\,km} closes on
impulse but is power-bound at the mean (39\,mW) -- closure there relies on
thrusting preferentially when the plasma is dense and the charging tax low,
which the float servo does without being told; \textbf{400\,km does not
close} for this body -- the demand exceeds the emission ceiling 63\% of the
time -- and we state that plainly rather than average around it. Two
honesty flags travel with the table. First, the corridor's boundary
behaviour is set by the night-side rows -- thin, cold plasma where the
collection stiffness $\propto 1/(n_e\sqrt{T_e})$ drives the float toward
the 50\,V limit -- and these are exactly the rows the PIC evidence has not
measured: the collection law's density axis is theory-anchored at one
dayside row. A single fixed-voltage run at a night-row density
($\sim$$2\times10^{11}$\,m$^{-3}$) is the targeted measurement that either
validates the corridor's edge or moves it, and is the first item of future
work. Second, power here is beam power $I\,V$; converter and cathode
overheads belong to the flight design and are not modelled.

\subsection{The geometry lever: slender bodies}
\label{sec:slender}
The mission table above holds the body shape fixed, but drag and collection
scale with \emph{different} areas: drag buys the ram silhouette, while
collection and solar harvest buy the total skin. A slender body flying
end-on decouples them. A \O10\,mm\,$\times$\,100\,mm cylinder presents the
same end cap as the chipsat -- the same nanonewton-class drag -- while its
lateral skin ($\sim$31\,cm$^2$, ten times the chipsat's total surface)
collects $\sim$46\,\textmu A of bare thermal current before any float
enhancement and carries enough solar cell area to push harvest from
$\sim$30 toward 100--200\,mW, at a mass of tens of grams. The niche
therefore extends up-mass along the rod (or plate) direction, not the cube:
payload, power, and return current grow with length while the drag bill
does not, and 400\,km -- infeasible for the chipsat in
Table~\ref{tab:missions} -- reopens for slender geometries on the power
side.

The concern this raised was that the collection exponent is provably
geometry-dependent. OML theory gives $\alpha = 1$ for a sphere and
$\alpha = 0.5$ for a long cylinder; our squat can measured 0.82--0.89,
between the limits, and a slender body should slide toward the cylindrical
value -- weaker enhancement per volt of float, so a hidden charging tax that
the area argument conceals. We therefore pre-registered two competing
hypotheses and measured them: area-only scaling (the can's fitted $\alpha$
holds, so tripling the skin drops the enhancement demand and the float lands
at 4--5\,V) against a cylinder-limit lateral wall (float in the tens of
volts, possibly past the benign gate).

The measurement settles it. A \O10\,mm\,$\times$\,30.5\,mm can
($L/r = 6$, skin $3.24\times$ the chipsat's) at the identical 200\,V drive,
commanded current, plasma row, grid and seed floats to
$\phi = 4.38$\,V tail-averaged (4.79\,V at run end, still rising at
$+4.4$\,mV/ns, so $\sim$5--6\,V settled), against the area arithmetic's
4.14--4.66\,V. Hypothesis A is confirmed and the cylinder-limit hypothesis
refuted by an order of magnitude: \textbf{the fitted exponent survives a
$3.24\times$ area change and an aspect-ratio change from $L/r = 0.6$ to 6.}
All six required gates pass; escape holds at 98.4\,\%.

Better still, elongation is not paid for in thrust. Because
$\mathrm{KE} = \kappa(V-\phi)$, a body that floats $3.9\times$ lower keeps
more of its drive: exhaust energy rises from $\sim$147 to 159.7\,eV and beam
thrust from 13.6 to 14.22\,nN, at the same commanded current and the same
ram silhouette. Elongation is free on the thrust side and strictly
favourable on the charging side.

Two design trades still travel with the lever: solar cells and bare
collector compete for the same wall area (clad dielectric surfaces do not
collect), and end-on flight is not attitude-free (gravity gradient prefers
the long axis nadir-pointed, which is side-on to ram). A third rule is fixed
by the measured gun physics: the emission ceiling scales as $1/d^{2}$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:frontier}), so a slender body must keep the
cathode--aperture gap at its short design value and grow the body
\emph{around} the gun -- an emitter placed at the far end of a long can
starves and scatters its own beam before the slit. We enforced this with a
cathode standoff that pins the gap while the can grows; the run's
demand-to-ceiling ratio, 1.457, matches the anchor's 1.46.

What remains extrapolation is aspect ratio \emph{beyond} $L/r = 6$, where
the cylinder limit must eventually bite, and the $r \lesssim \lambda_D$ end
that converges to the bare-tether collection law of
Sanmart\'in et al.~\cite{san93} grounding our related work.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}
We state plainly where this study is known to be incomplete, so a reader can
judge exactly how far the present results extend. This is a
feasibility-concept paper: the device physics is measured under the evidence
contract of Sec.~\ref{sec:method}; the mission projection is model-based
with flagged extrapolation; full mission validation is future work.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The collection law's density axis is unmeasured.} All three
        frontier runs share one dayside plasma row; the
        $n_e\sqrt{T_e}$ scaling under the fitted $(1+\chi)^\alpha$ law is
        standard sheath theory but no committed run has confirmed it at
        another density. The model flags every such row
        (Table~\ref{tab:missions}); the night-row run is the single
        highest-value follow-on measurement.
  \item \textbf{The collection law's geometry axis is measured at two
        points, not mapped.} The exponent is geometry-dependent by theory
        (sphere 1, long cylinder 0.5; measured 0.82--0.89 for the can), and
        the pre-registered slender run (Sec.~\ref{sec:slender}) confirms it
        survives to $L/r = 6$ and $3.24\times$ skin area. Aspect ratios
        beyond that, and bodies whose radius approaches $\lambda_D$, remain
        extrapolation resting on the theory-safe thermal-collection floor
        plus flags, not on the fitted law.
  \item \textbf{Grid resolution is the leading numerical uncertainty.}
        Particle count is converged to $\le$0.05\% (this work) and time
        step and seed to $\le$0.5\% (predecessor), but the measured
        0.15$\to$0.10\,mm grid shift is $+4.0$\% on thrust and $+7.4$\% on
        exhaust energy (matched-window protocol,
        Sec.~\ref{sec:credibility}), carried as the error band on every
        frontier number. Two grid points cannot establish an asymptotic
        order; a third is future work. The shift's sign is conservative
        for all acceptance gates.
  \item \textbf{Finite-time equilibrium on the ion clock.} The 300\,V float
        was still relaxing at run end (Sec.~\ref{sec:collection}); the
        settled-value band (42--48\,V) is carried through the $\alpha$ fit
        rather than hidden, and brushes the 50\,V design limit.
  \item \textbf{Reduced ion mass.} Simulations use $400\,m_e$ rather than
        real O$^+$ ($29{,}166\,m_e$) to keep ion timescales affordable; the
        sensitivity of the float to ion mass has not been measured.
  \item \textbf{Stationary ambient plasma.} No bulk flow is modelled. For
        electron collection the ram anisotropy is small
        ($v_{\mathrm{orb}}/v_{\mathrm{th},e} \approx 5\%$); for a body
        flying end-on the ram-ion wake is the largest unmodelled effect and
        is deferred, not estimated.
  \item \textbf{Two-dimensional axisymmetry, no geomagnetic field.} A real
        flight geometry sees a transverse geomagnetic field component this
        model omits.
  \item \textbf{System power.} All powers are beam power $I\,V$. Cathode
        heating (zero for field emitters, nonzero otherwise) and converter
        efficiency are flight-design quantities outside this paper's scope.
  \item \textbf{Full mission simulation.} Battery state of charge, seasonal
        density variation, attitude coupling, and a flight-software
        implementation of the servo are future work; the model's per-row
        accounting (Sec.~\ref{sec:application}) is the input such a study
        needs, not a substitute for it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Drag compensation for gram-class spacecraft at 450--600\,km requires
nanonewtons of continuous thrust from milliwatts of power -- a slot no
flight propulsion system occupies: the smallest controllable flight EP sits
two to three orders of magnitude above the demand, in packages heavier than
the entire spacecraft. We have measured, under a pre-registered evidence
contract, the complete thrust--power frontier of a device that fills the
slot with zero propellant and zero net mass flux: a sealed conducting body,
a cold electron gun, and the ionosphere as both propellant reservoir and
return electrode. Three results support treating this as a credible
propulsion mechanism rather than a numerical curiosity. First, the measured
frontier -- 3.4\,nN at 12\,mW to 30\,nN at 189\,mW across the full hardware
voltage range -- confirms the $F/P \propto 1/\sqrt{V}$ throttle law within
2.5\% and delivers ion-thruster-class energy conversion
($\approx$0.73) at a thrust-per-watt $\sim$200$\times$ below gridded ion:
the pair of numbers that assigns this device the nanonewton regime and
nothing above it. Second, the returning current does not cancel the
thrust: a closed momentum ledger with a dedicated full-return null
experiment establishes it in the predecessor implementation, and per-run
momentum gates bound the return channel at 1--9\% of beam thrust in the
present one. Third, the measured collection law collapses optimal
operation into a two-line servo on the spacecraft's own floating potential,
and the resulting model, applied to year-long orbit propagations with every
extrapolated regime flagged, bounds a mission corridor: closure on
harvested power at 550--600\,km, impulse closure at 500\,km, honest failure
at 400\,km. The device is inexpensive and low-risk to test -- no hardware
beyond a high-voltage supply and an electron gun of a kind that already
flies as a neutralizer -- which suggests both a rapid path to an on-orbit
demonstration and a retrofit use for existing propulsion systems after
their propellant is exhausted.
